\subsection*{Task 1.1 -- Data Augmentation}

The \texttt{vanilla} branch of \texttt{get\_data\_loader} composes the
following pipeline (in order): resize to a slightly larger canvas, take a
random square crop back to \texttt{opts.image\_size}, randomly flip
horizontally, convert to a tensor in $[0,1]$, then normalise to $[-1,1]$
so the output range matches the generator's \texttt{tanh}.

\begin{lstlisting}
load_size = int(1.1 * opts.image_size)
osize = [load_size, load_size]
train_transform = transforms.Compose([
    transforms.Resize(osize, Image.BICUBIC),
    transforms.RandomCrop(opts.image_size),
    transforms.RandomHorizontalFlip(),
    transforms.ToTensor(),
    transforms.Normalize((0.5, 0.5, 0.5), (0.5, 0.5, 0.5)),
])
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Task 1.2a -- Padding Derivation}

For a 2-D convolution with input spatial size $H_{\text{in}}$, kernel
size $K$, stride $S$, and padding $P$, the output size is
%
\begin{equation}
    H_{\text{out}}
    \;=\;
    \left\lfloor
    \frac{H_{\text{in}} + 2P - K}{S}
    \right\rfloor + 1.
    \label{eq:conv-out}
\end{equation}
%
The discriminator's first four convolutions must downsample by exactly a
factor of two, i.e. $H_{\text{out}} = H_{\text{in}}/2$, with the prescribed
$K = 4$ and $S = 2$. Substituting into~\eqref{eq:conv-out} (and noting
that $H_{\text{in}}$ is even so the floor is exact):
%
\begin{align*}
    \frac{H_{\text{in}}}{2}
        &= \frac{H_{\text{in}} + 2P - 4}{2} + 1 \\[2pt]
    H_{\text{in}}
        &= H_{\text{in}} + 2P - 4 + 2 \\[2pt]
    0   &= 2P - 2 \\[2pt]
    \boxed{P = 1.}
\end{align*}
%
Sanity check on layer~1: $H_{\text{in}}=64$ gives
$\lfloor (64 + 2 - 4)/2 \rfloor + 1 = 31 + 1 = 32$, halving the spatial
extent as required. The same holds for the remaining three downsampling
layers.

\subsection*{Task 1.3 -- DCGenerator}

The generator is the discriminator's mirror image: it takes a noise
vector $z\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{\,\text{noise\_size}}$ shaped as
$(\text{noise\_size}, 1, 1)$ and progressively upsamples it to a
$3\!\times\!64\!\times\!64$ image. Layers~2--5 follow the canonical
``upsample then convolve'' recipe: nearest-neighbour upsampling with
\verb|scale_factor=2| doubles the spatial extent, and a
$K\!=\!3, S\!=\!1, P\!=\!1$ convolution preserves it (verifying with
\eqref{eq:conv-out}: $\lfloor(2H_{\text{in}}+2-3)/1\rfloor+1 = 2H_{\text{in}}$).
This factored design avoids the well-known checkerboard artifacts that
plain transposed convolutions can produce.

\paragraph{First layer: $1\!\times\!1 \to 4\!\times\!4$ with no upsample.}
Following the assignment hint, \verb|up_conv1| is built with the
\verb|conv()| helper (i.e.\ a plain \verb|Conv2d| with InstanceNorm and
ReLU, \emph{no} \verb|Upsample| stage at all) and lets the convolution
itself expand the spatial extent. From~\eqref{eq:conv-out} with
$H_{\text{in}}=1$, $H_{\text{out}}=4$, $S=1$:
\begin{align*}
    4 &= (1 + 2P - K) + 1 \\[2pt]
    \Rightarrow\; 2P - K &= 2.
\end{align*}
Choosing $K=4$ gives $\boxed{P=3}$. Sanity check: padding the
$1\!\times\!1$ input on every side with three zeros yields a
$7\!\times\!7$ tensor; sliding a $4\!\times\!4$ kernel with stride~1
produces $\lfloor (7-4)/1 \rfloor + 1 = 4$ output positions, as required.

        \midrule

        up\_conv2 & $256 \to 128$                & 3 & 1 & 1 & $\times 2$ & InstanceNorm & ReLU \\

             \texttt{Normalize((0.5,...),(0.5,...))} step in Task~1.1.}

\subsection*{Task 1.4 -- DCGAN Training Loop}

The training loop alternates one discriminator step and one generator
step per minibatch, both with the least-squares loss from
Algorithm~1 of the assignment.

\paragraph{Discriminator update.} Given $m$ real images $x$ and $m$
generator samples $\tilde{x}=G(z)$ (with fresh noise $z\!\sim\!\mathcal{U}[-1,1]^{\,d}$),
\begin{align*}
    J^{(D)}_{\text{real}} &= \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(D(x^{(i)}) - 1\bigr)^{2}, \\
    J^{(D)}_{\text{fake}} &= \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} D\bigl(G(z^{(i)})\bigr)^{2}, \\
    J^{(D)}                  &= \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(J^{(D)}_{\text{real}} + J^{(D)}_{\text{fake}}\bigr).
\end{align*}
The fake batch is detached (\verb|fake_images.detach()|) before the
discriminator forward pass so the discriminator gradients do not leak
into $G$'s parameters.

\paragraph{Generator update.} A \emph{fresh} noise batch $z'$ is drawn,
$\tilde{x}'=G(z')$ is generated, and
\[
    J^{(G)} \;=\; \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(D(G(z'^{(i)})) - 1\bigr)^{2}.
\]
Here gradients must flow through both $D$ and $G$, so no detach is used
and only the generator parameters are stepped. Drawing new noise (rather
than reusing the discriminator's batch) is what the pseudocode
prescribes and avoids any subtle bias from over-fitting to the same
samples in the same iteration.

\subsection*{Task 1.5 -- Differentiable Augmentation}

\verb|prepare_images| is the hook the training loop calls on every batch
of images before they reach the discriminator (both real and fake). With
\verb|--use_diffaug| it applies the DiffAugment policy
\verb|'color,translation,cutout'|; otherwise it is a pass-through:

\begin{lstlisting}
def prepare_images(images, opts):
    if opts.use_diffaug:
        return DiffAugment(images, policy=policy)
    return images
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Why this is different from Task 1.1.} Standard data
augmentation (Task 1.1) lives in the dataloader, so it only touches
\emph{real} images and is non-differentiable -- no gradient ever flows
through it. DiffAugment is applied \emph{inside} the training loop to
both real and fake images, and every transformation it uses
(brightness/colour jitter, translation, cutout) is implemented with
differentiable PyTorch ops. As a result, when the generator's loss is
back-propagated through the discriminator, gradients continue to flow
through the augmentation into $G$. This is what the original
DiffAugment paper identifies as essential: if only $D$ saw augmented
images while $G$ was trained against the un-augmented version, the two
networks would be optimising different distributions and training would
degrade.

\subsection*{Task 1.6 -- W\&B Logging}

Logging is wired up at four sites in \verb|vanilla_gan.py|:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Run init} (top of \verb|training_loop|, after the
          fixed-noise vector is created):
\begin{lstlisting}
wandb.init(
    project='assignment1-dcgan',
    name=f'dcgan_diffaug={opts.use_diffaug}',
    config=vars(opts),
)
\end{lstlisting}
          The run name carries the boolean \verb|opts.use_diffaug| so
          the with/without comparison is immediate in the dashboard,
          and \verb|config=vars(opts)| serialises every command-line
          argument.

    \item \textbf{Scalar losses} every \verb|opts.log_step| iterations:
          \verb|D/real_loss|, \verb|D/fake_loss|, \verb|D/total_loss|,
          \verb|G/loss|, all keyed by \verb|step=iteration|. The
          \verb|D/| and \verb|G/| prefixes group the four panels under
          ``Discriminator'' and ``Generator'' headers in W\&B.

    \item \textbf{Image grid} inside \verb|save_samples| (every
          \verb|opts.sample_every| iterations) using
          \verb|wandb.Image(grid, caption=f'iter {iteration}')|. The
          grid is the same uint8 image that is also written to disk, so
          the dashboard sample matches the local artefact bit-for-bit.

    \item \textbf{Run finish}: \verb|wandb.finish()| after the training
          loop returns.
\end{enumerate}

Both \verb|wandb.log| calls pass \verb|step=iteration|, so the
loss-curve panel and the image panel share a common monotone time axis
that lets the visual quality at iteration $k$ be read off against the
loss values at the same $k$.

\subsection*{Task 1.7 -- Experiments \& Report}

The DCGAN was trained for 500 epochs both with and without
\verb|--use_diffaug| (otherwise identical hyperparameters: batch size
16, $\eta=2\!\times\!10^{-4}$, Adam $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.5,0.999)$,
\verb|--data_preprocess vanilla|). All loss curves, image grids, and
the discussion required by the assignment (loss-curve evolution,
visual-quality progression, and the Task~1.1 vs Task~1.5 augmentation
comparison) are collected in a public W\&B Report:

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\centering
\textbf{W\&B Report:}\\
\url{https://api.wandb.ai/links/roimoshe1995-tel-aviv-university/0n0y7jgw}
}}
\end{center}

The report visibility is set to \emph{Anyone with the link can view}.

\paragraph{Loss-curve evolution.}
Both runs were trained for $\sim\!6{,}500$ iterations
($500$ epochs $\times \sim\!13$ iters/epoch on the $\sim\!200$-image
\texttt{grumpifyBprocessed} dataset, batch size~$16$). Under the
least-squares loss, the LSGAN equilibrium $D\!\equiv\!0.5$ corresponds
to $D/\text{real}\!=\!D/\text{fake}\!=\!D/\text{total}\!=\!G\!=\!0.25$,
while a fully discriminator-dominated regime
($D(x)\!\to\!1$, $D(G(z))\!\to\!0$) gives $D/\text{total}\!\to\!0$ and
$G\!\to\!1$.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item The \textbf{baseline} run sits squarely in the
          discriminator-dominated regime: $D/\text{total}$ settles
          around $\sim\!0.04$ and even drifts slightly downward,
          $D/\text{real}$ and $D/\text{fake}$ both hover at
          $\sim\!0.03$--$0.05$, and $G/\text{loss}$ saturates near
          $\sim\!1.0$. With only $\sim\!200$ training images and no
          augmentation inside the training loop, the discriminator
          quickly memorises the dataset and the generator's gradient
          signal collapses -- the textbook small-data DCGAN failure
          mode the assignment warns about in Section~1.1.
    \item The \textbf{DiffAugment} run is markedly closer to
          equilibrium: $D/\text{total}\!\approx\!0.15$,
          $G/\text{loss}\!\approx\!0.65$, both stationary throughout
          training. The visible upward spikes in $D/\text{real}$ and
          $D/\text{fake}$ (occasional excursions to $0.3$--$0.5$) are
          the expected consequence of randomised augmentations
          presenting batch-to-batch variation in difficulty -- they
          are a feature, since they prevent the discriminator from
          locking onto memorised features. Crucially, $D/\text{total}$
          shows no downward trend.
\end{itemize}
Neither run reaches the ideal $0.25$ equilibrium, which is consistent
with the very small dataset and the absence of additional regularisers,
but DiffAugment moves both networks substantially in the right
direction.

\paragraph{Visual quality over training.}
Inspecting the \texttt{samples} grid at three checkpoints (early
$\sim\!200$, mid $\sim\!2400$, late $\sim\!6400$):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Iter $\sim\!200$.} Both runs produce coarse
          brown/black/white blobs with no recognisable structure.
          Indistinguishable from each other -- training has not yet
          had time to differentiate the two regimes.
    \item \textbf{Iter $\sim\!2400$.} Both runs have learned the
          Grumpy-Cat face structure (eyes, ears, mouth in roughly the
          right places). The \emph{baseline} grid already exhibits
          the classic small-data pathologies: purple/magenta
          chromatic noise around the eyes and ears and high
          uniformity across the 16 cells. The \emph{DiffAugment} grid
          is softer, with more natural fur and background colours
          and visible cell-to-cell variation in pose and lighting.
    \item \textbf{Iter $\sim\!6400$.} Both runs sharpen further. The
          \emph{baseline} samples now show pronounced cyan/magenta
          fringing along facial outlines and an ``etched'',
          over-sharpened texture; the \emph{DiffAugment} samples have
          cleaner colours, smoother fur, and more cell-to-cell
          variation in head tilt and shading.
\end{itemize}
Because the dataset is essentially photographs of a single cat, the
modest diversity gap between the two runs cannot be read as full mode
collapse; the more telling differences are the \emph{artefact
patterns}. The baseline's chromatic fringing and over-sharpening are
exactly what one would predict from the loss curves: with $D$ winning
decisively, $G$'s gradient saturates, so $G$ keeps pushing
high-frequency texture without correcting any of the colour or shape
errors $D$ no longer cares about.

\paragraph{Standard dataset augmentation vs.\ DiffAugment.}
The two augmentation schemes used in this assignment differ along
three axes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Where applied.} The Task~1.1 transform lives inside
          the \texttt{Dataset} (\verb|data_loader.py|) and is applied
          \emph{only} to real images, once per item, before they enter
          the training loop. DiffAugment (Task~1.5) is applied
          \emph{inside} the training loop via \verb|prepare_images|,
          to \emph{both} real images and the generator's fake images,
          before they reach the discriminator.
    \item \textbf{Differentiability.} \verb|RandomCrop| and
          \verb|RandomHorizontalFlip| are non-differentiable PIL
          operations -- this is harmless, because they only ever touch
          real images that the loss does not back-propagate through.
          DiffAugment's transformations (random brightness, colour
          jitter, translation, cutout) are implemented with
          differentiable PyTorch ops, so when the generator's loss
          $\bigl(D(\text{aug}(G(z)))-1\bigr)^{2}$ is back-propagated,
          gradients flow through the augmentation back into $G$.
          This is essential: if augmentations were applied only to $D$
          but were not differentiable, $G$ would be optimised against
          a discriminator trained on a different distribution from the
          one $G$ actually sees evaluating itself, and training would
          drift.
    \item \textbf{Effect on stability and output.} In our experiment
          the Task~1.1 augmentation alone was insufficient: the
          baseline run saw $D/\text{total}$ collapse to
          $\sim\!0.04$ and produced samples with strong chromatic
          artefacts. Adding DiffAugment kept $D/\text{total}$
          stationary at $\sim\!0.15$, brought $G/\text{loss}$ down
          from $\sim\!1.0$ to $\sim\!0.65$, and produced visibly
          cleaner samples with less fringing and more diversity. The
          two schemes are complementary rather than competing:
          standard augmentation grows the effective dataset cheaply
          for free at load time, while DiffAugment regularises the
          adversarial game itself by ensuring $D$ never sees the same
          batch twice.
\end{itemize}
